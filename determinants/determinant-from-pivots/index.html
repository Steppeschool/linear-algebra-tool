Strang §5.1 · Determinants by elimination

Eliminate, then multiply the pivots.

Elimination does not change the determinant, and a triangular matrix has an easy determinant: the product of its diagonal. So det A is the product of the pivots, with a minus sign for each row exchange. This is how computers find determinants.

Lesson 6 · slide 3Det from pivots
det A = ± (pivot₁ × pivot₂ × ... × pivotₙ)    det AB = det A · det B    det Aᵀ = det A
Key ideas
  1. Elimination steps (subtract a multiple of a row) keep det the same.
  2. Each row exchange flips the sign.
  3. For a triangular matrix, multiply down the diagonal.
  4. So det A = ± the product of the pivots. A zero pivot that no swap can fix means det A = 0.
  5. Two more facts: det AB = det A · det B, and det Aᵀ = det A.

Squaring up a box

To measure the volume of a slanted box, slide its layers until it stands upright. Sliding layers (shears) does not change the volume. An upright box is easy: multiply its three side lengths.

Elimination is that sliding. It turns A into an upper triangular U without changing the determinant, and the "side lengths" of U are its diagonal entries, the pivots.

Why the diagonal is enough

For a triangular matrix, use rule 3 to pull each diagonal entry out of its row. What is left has 1s on the diagonal, and more elimination clears the rest to give I, with det I = 1. So det U is just the product of the diagonal. (If a diagonal entry is 0, there is no pivot in that row: elimination produces a zero row and det U = 0, which is still the product of the diagonal.)

A worked example

A = 120251013
Row 2 − 2·row 1: (0, 1, 1).   Then row 3 − 1·row 2: (0, 0, 2)
U = 120011002,   pivots 1, 1, 2
det A = 1 · 1 · 2 = 2 ✓ (no row exchanges)

The 3 by 3 matrix K = 2114−60−272 from lesson 2 had pivots 2, −8 and 1, so its determinant is 2 · (−8) · 1 = −16.
