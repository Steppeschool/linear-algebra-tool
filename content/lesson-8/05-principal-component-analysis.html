Principal component analysis finds those directions. Subtract the average, put the data in a matrix, and take its SVD: the first right singular vector points along the cloud, and σ₁² says how much of the spread it captures. center: C = data − mean    C = UΣVᵀ    first principal component = v₁    share = σ₁² / Σσᵢ²
  1. Put the measurements as rows of a matrix and subtract the average of each column: the centered matrix C.
  2. The first principal component is v₁, the first right singular vector of C: the direction of greatest spread.
  3. σᵢ² measures the spread along vᵢ; σ₁² / (σ₁² + σ₂² + …) is the share it explains.
  4. The line through the mean along v₁ minimises the sum of squared perpendicular distances to the points.

A robot vacuum along a wall

A robot vacuum drives along a straight wall and logs its (x, y) position eight times. The tracker is noisy, so the points scatter a little around the true path. Which direction is the wall?

Look at the cloud: it is long in one direction and thin across. The long direction is the wall; the thin direction is the noise. PCA finds the long direction from the numbers.

Step 1: center the data

The average position is (3.94, 2.71). Subtract it from every point. The cloud now sits around the origin, and only its shape matters. Put the centered points as the rows of an 8 by 2 matrix C.

Step 2: the SVD of C

For a unit direction v, Cv lists how far each point reaches along v, and |Cv|² adds up the squares: the spread along v. Slide 1 says |Cv| is largest for v = v₁, with |Cv₁| = σ₁. So v₁ is the direction of greatest spread: the first principal component.

For the robot data: v₁ points at about 29.6°, the second direction v₂ is perpendicular to it
σ₁² / (σ₁² + σ₂²) ≈ 0.992: the wall direction explains 99.2% of the spread

Not the same as least squares

Lesson 5 fitted a line by making the vertical gaps small: it assumed t was exact and only b was noisy. PCA treats x and y the same and makes the perpendicular distances small. Here both coordinates are noisy, so PCA is the right tool. (Spread along v₁ plus squared distances to the line is the fixed total, so making one big makes the other small.)

Where this is used

PCA is the first step in most data work: it shrinks hundreds of sensor channels to a few meaningful directions, finds the main axis of an IMU's vibration, and compresses data before it is sent over a slow radio link. In 3D, the first two components of a scanned surface give its best-fitting plane.

Example 1 · Points exactly on a line

(0, 0), (1, 2), (2, 4), centered: (−1, −2), (0, 0), (1, 2).

C = −1−20012: rank 1
v₁ = (1, 2)/√5, σ₂ = 0: 100% of the spread along the line

Example 2 · A round cloud

Points (1, 0), (−1, 0), (0, 1), (0, −1): already centered.

CᵀC = 2002: σ₁ = σ₂ = √2
Every direction explains 50%: no preferred direction

Example 3 · Spread along an axis

Centered points (−3, 0.1), (3, −0.1), (0, 0).

v₁ is almost (1, 0): the x-direction holds nearly all the spread (18 against 0.02).

1

Why must the data be centered before taking the SVD?

Show solutionHide solution

Otherwise v₁ points towards the cloud's position (from the origin to its centre), not along its shape. Centering removes the position.

2

The singular values of centered data are 10 and 1. What share of the spread does the first component explain?

Show solutionHide solution

100 / (100 + 1) ≈ 99%.

3

Three sensors measure nearly the same thing. What do you expect the singular values of the centered data to look like?

Show solutionHide solution

One large σ₁ and two small ones: the readings move together along one direction, with small independent noise.
