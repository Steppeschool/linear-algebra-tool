A matrix is the short way to write them. Think of the matrix A as a list of allowed steps, one step in each column. The vector x says how many of each step to take. Then Ax is where you end up. So Ax is nothing new: it is slide 1's combination, written in two letters. a₁₁a₁₂a₂₁a₂₂ x₁x₂ = x₁ a₁₁a₂₁ + x₂ a₁₂a₂₂
  1. A matrix is a block of numbers. Its columns are vectors.
  2. Ax is a combination of the columns of A, with the entries of x as the weights.
  3. Each entry of Ax is also a dot product of a row of A with x: multiply matching entries and add. Both ways give the same numbers.
  4. Columns tell us what Ax means. Rows are how we usually compute it by hand.

From combinations to matrices

Writing out c(2, 1) + d(−1, 1) every time gets long. So we put the vectors in the columns of a matrix A, and c and d in a vector x:

A = 2−111    x = (c, d)    Ax = c·(2, 1) + d·(−1, 1)

(2, 1) is written across to save space, but it still means a column: 2 on top, 1 below.

aij is the entry in row i, column j, so a₁₂ is in row 1, column 2. And x = (x₁, x₂): x₁ is the c and x₂ is the d from slide 1.

The definition: Ax is x₁ times column 1, plus x₂ times column 2. The colours match the arrows in the lab below.

An everyday picture: a shopping basket

An egg has about 6 g of protein and 1 g of carbohydrate. A bowl of rice has about 4 g and 45 g. Each food is a column:

A = 64145    x = (2 eggs, 1 bowl)

The basket holds 2·(6, 1) + 1·(4, 45) = (16, 47): 16 g of protein, 47 g of carbohydrate. That total is Ax. Adding whole foods is the column way.

The row way gives the same answer

Or add one nutrient at a time. Protein: 6·2 + 4·1 = 16. Carbohydrate: 1·2 + 45·1 = 47. Each is a dot product of a row of A with x.

Both ways add the same products, so they always agree. Rows are quicker by hand. Columns show what Ax can be.

Watch the sizes

A vector with n entries is an n by 1 matrix. x needs one entry per column of A, and Ax has one entry per row. So a 2 by 3 matrix (2 rows, 3 columns) takes in 3 numbers and gives back 2.

(2 × 3) · (3 × 1) → (2 × 1)    the inner numbers must match, the outer numbers give the size of the answer

Next: So one x gives one output Ax. Next: try every x and collect all the outputs. That set tells us which targets b we can hit.

Example 1 · Both ways

A = 3125, x = (1, 2).

By columns: 1·(3, 2) + 2·(1, 5) = (5, 12)
By rows: (3·1 + 1·2, 2·1 + 5·2) = (5, 12)

Example 2 · A wide matrix

A = 102311 has 3 columns, so x needs 3 entries. Take x = (2, 1, 1). (2 × 3) · (3 × 1) → (2 × 1)

Ax = 2·(1, 3) + 1·(0, 1) + 1·(2, 1) = (4, 8)

Three columns in, a vector with 2 entries out: one per row of A.

Example 3 · Picking out a column

What is A times x = (1, 0)? And times (0, 1)?

A(1, 0) = 1·(column 1) + 0·(column 2) = column 1
A(0, 1) = column 2

So the columns of A are exactly what A does to (1, 0) and (0, 1). We use this again on slide 5.

Example 4 · Two motors on a drone

Look at a drone from behind and keep only two motors, left and right. We track two numbers: the lift (the total upward push) and the tilt (right push minus left push; positive means the right side rises). Each motor gives one column:

M = 11−11   column 1: left motor, column 2: right motor
Both motors at 2:   M(2, 2) = 2·(1, −1) + 2·(1, 1) = (4, 0)
Left 1, right 3:   M(1, 3) = 1·(1, −1) + 3·(1, 1) = (4, 2)

The lift stays 4, so the drone holds its height, but now the right side rises. The output is a mix of the motor columns, weighted by the thrusts.
