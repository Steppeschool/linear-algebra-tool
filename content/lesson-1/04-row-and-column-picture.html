Now let's find it. You can read two equations in two ways. By rows: each equation is a line on the map, a clue that says “the answer is somewhere on me”. The answer is where both clues agree, where the two lines cross. By columns: how many of each step must we walk to reach b? Both readings give the same answer. 1-232 xy = 111
  1. Row picture: each equation is a line. The solution is where the lines meet.
  2. Column picture: the system says x·(column 1) + y·(column 2) = b. Here x and y are plain numbers, the two entries of the unknown u = (x, y). The solution gives the right weights.
  3. Both pictures give the same solution (x, y). They also fail together: parallel lines go with dependent columns.
  4. In higher dimensions the column picture stays easy to think about, so we lean on it.

One system, two readings

Take Professor Strang's first example: x − 2y = 1 and 3x + 2y = 11. As a matrix equation it is Au = b, with unknown u = (x, y):

A = 1−232    b = (1, 11)

The row picture: two clues

Each equation is a line, and each says "the answer is on me". x − 2y = 1 is one line. 3x + 2y = 11 is another. The answer is where they cross: (3, 1). Check: 3 − 2 = 1 and 9 + 2 = 11.

The column picture: a recipe

Now read it by columns, as on slide 2:

x·(1, 3) + y·(−2, 2) = (1, 11)
3·(1, 3) + 1·(−2, 2) = (3 − 2, 9 + 2) = (1, 11) ✓

Now we ask how much of each column makes b. Same answer: x = 3, y = 1.

When the pictures go wrong

  • Parallel lines: they never meet, so there is no solution. The columns are dependent and b is off their line.
  • The same line twice: infinitely many solutions. b is on the line of the columns.

Why do the two go together? Lines are parallel when one left side is a multiple of the other, like x + y and 2x + 2y. Then both columns look like (n, 2n), here (1, 2) and (1, 2): one line.

Why the column picture wins later

With three unknowns, each equation is a plane in space, and three planes are hard to draw. The column picture stays simple: three arrows and one target.

To solve by hand, though, we use rows. That is elimination, in lesson 2.

Next: So far we fed A one input and looked at one output. The next slide changes the view: feed A every point of the plane at once, and watch the whole plane move.

Example 1 · One crossing

x + y = 3 and x − y = 1.

Rows: the lines cross at (2, 1)
Columns: 2·(1, 1) + 1·(1, −1) = (3, 1) = b ✓

Example 2 · The same line twice

x + 2y = 4 and 2x + 4y = 8.

Rows: the second equation is 2 × the first, so both are one line
Columns: (1, 2) and (2, 4) are dependent, and b = (4, 8) lies on their line

Infinitely many solutions, for example (4, 0) and (0, 2).

Example 3 · No crossing at all

x + y = 2 and 2x + 2y = 5.

Rows: the second line is x + y = 2.5, parallel to the first. They never meet
Columns: (1, 2) and (1, 2) are the same vector, and b = (2, 5) is not a multiple of (1, 2)

Both pictures agree: no solution.
