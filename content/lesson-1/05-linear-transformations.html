  1. A matrix sends each vector v to a new vector Av. Applied to every point, it moves the whole plane.
  2. Lines stay straight, evenly spaced lines stay evenly spaced, and the origin stays fixed.
  3. Column 1 is where (1, 0) lands. Column 2 is where (0, 1) lands. Those two facts decide everything.
  4. The area of any shape is multiplied by |det A|.

Think of graph paper

Pin a sheet of graph paper at the origin. You may stretch it, turn it, slant it, or flip it over. You may not bend it, fold it or tear it. Every 2 by 2 matrix does some mix of these motions. A singular matrix, one with determinant 0 (the number ps − qr from slide 3), can also squash the paper flat onto a line.

Why can't a matrix bend the paper? Because matrices respect combinations: A(cv + dw) = cAv + dAw. The reason is the row way from slide 2: each entry is a row times the vector, and ordinary algebra gives row·(cv + dw) = c(row·v) + d(row·w). Points that were evenly spaced along a straight line stay evenly spaced along a straight line.

Two landmarks decide everything

Every point can be written as a combination of the two basic vectors: (x, y) = x·(1, 0) + y·(0, 1). Since A respects combinations,

A(x, y) = x·A(1, 0) + y·A(0, 1) = x·(column 1) + y·(column 2)

This is slide 2 again, seen in a new way. If we know where the two landmarks (1, 0) and (0, 1) go, we know where every point goes.

That also tells us how to build a matrix: decide where (1, 0) and (0, 1) should land, and write those two vectors as the columns.

Building a rotation

To turn the plane by an angle θ (counterclockwise), follow the two landmarks around the unit circle. (1, 0) sits at angle 0°, so it lands at angle θ: the point (cos θ, sin θ). (0, 1) sits at 90°, so it lands at angle 90° + θ: the point (cos(90° + θ), sin(90° + θ)) = (−sin θ, cos θ). Those are the columns:

Q = cos θ−sin θsin θcos θ
For a quarter turn, θ = 90°: cos θ = 0 and sin θ = 1, so Q = 0−110. It sends (1, 0) to (0, 1).

What happens to areas

Watch the shaded unit square in the lab. The matrix turns it into a parallelogram, and the area of that parallelogram is |det A| = |ps − qr|, with p, q, r, s named as on slide 3. Every other shape has its area multiplied by the same number (Strang §5.3).

The sign of det A says one more thing. A negative determinant means the plane has been flipped over, like graph paper turned face down. Slide 7 builds such a flip.

A rotation has det Q = cos²θ + sin²θ = 1. It turns the paper without stretching it.

Where this is used

An IMU measures acceleration along the drone's own axes. A 3 by 3 rotation matrix turns those readings into north, east and up. An attitude estimator spends its time keeping that matrix up to date.

Example 1 · Stretch sideways

Triple every x-coordinate, keep y: (1, 0) → (3, 0) and (0, 1) → (0, 1).

A = 3001    det A = 3
(1, 1) → (3, 1). Every area becomes 3 times larger

Example 2 · Half a turn

Rotate by 180°: cos 180° = −1, sin 180° = 0.

Q = −100−1    det Q = 1
(2, 1) → (−2, −1): every vector points the opposite way

Example 3 · Squash onto a line

Where does A = 1212 send the plane?

(1, 0) → (1, 1) and (0, 1) → (2, 2): both columns lie on the line y = x
det A = 1·2 − 2·1 = 0

Every point lands on the line y = x, and every area becomes 0. This is the dependent case from slide 3, seen as a motion.
