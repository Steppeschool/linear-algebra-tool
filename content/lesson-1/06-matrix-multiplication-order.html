(AB)v = A(Bv): B moves the plane first, then A moves the result. AB = A b₁b₂ = Ab₁Ab₂

Think: socks and shoes

Socks first, then shoes. Swap the order and you get a very different result. Matrix moves work the same way.

Read a product from right to left, like f(g(x)) in maths. In AB, B acts first.

Column by column

Where does (1, 0) end up after both moves? B sends it to b₁, the first column of B. Then A sends that to Ab₁. So the first column of AB is A times the first column of B. Same for the second column.

You already know matrix times vector. Matrix times matrix is just that, once per column.

Worked example

R turns by 90°. S is a shear.
R = 0-110   S = 1101
RS = 0-111   SR = 1-110
RS shears, then turns. SR turns, then shears. Different matrices, different moves. Look where the F ends up.

The rules

  • AB is usually not BA. Order matters.
  • (AB)C = A(BC). Either way, C acts first, then B, then A. Brackets only change how you write it.
  • det(AB) = det(A)·det(B). If B doubles areas and A triples them, together they multiply areas by 6 (chapter 5).

Where you meet this

A robot arm is a chain of rotations, one per joint. To find the gripper, multiply them in joint order. Swap two and the gripper ends up somewhere else.

Example 1 · Two orders

A = 1201 (shear), B = 2001 (stretch).

AB = 2201    BA = 2401

Stretch then shear differs from shear then stretch.

Example 2 · Two quarter turns

R = 0−110 turns by 90°.

RR = −100−1

Two quarter turns make a half turn. For rotations, multiplying adds the angles.
