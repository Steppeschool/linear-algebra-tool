Drop a perpendicular from b to the line. The error e = b − p is perpendicular to a, and that one fact gives the formula. p = x̂ a    x̂ = aᵀb / aᵀa    e = b − p ⟂ a
  1. The projection p lies on the line, so p = x̂a for some number x̂.
  2. The error e = b − p is perpendicular to a: aᵀ(b − x̂a) = 0.
  3. Solving for the number: x̂ = aᵀb / aᵀa.
  4. If b is on the line, p = b. If b is perpendicular to a, p = 0.

The shortest way to the road

You are standing in a field next to a long straight road and want to reach it with as few steps as possible. You do not walk at a slant. You walk straight at it, at a right angle. The spot where you arrive is the point of the road closest to you.

That is a projection. The road is the line through a, you are b, and the arrival point is p. The path you walked, e = b − p, makes a right angle with the road.

From the right angle to a formula

Two facts are enough:

  • p is on the line, so it is a multiple of a: p = x̂a. We only need the number x̂ (read "x-hat"; the hat marks the best choice).
  • e = b − x̂a is perpendicular to a, so its dot product with a is zero.
aᵀ(b − x̂a) = 0
aᵀb − x̂ aᵀa = 0
x̂ = aᵀb / aᵀa,    p = x̂a

Two dot products and one division. Nothing else is needed, in two dimensions or in a thousand.

A worked example

a = (2, 1),   b = (1, 3)
aᵀb = 2 + 3 = 5,   aᵀa = 4 + 1 = 5,   x̂ = 1
p = (2, 1),   e = b − p = (−1, 2)
check: a · e = −2 + 2 = 0 ✓

Why p is the closest point

Take any other point q on the line. The path from b to q is e plus a step along the line. Those two pieces are perpendicular, so by Pythagoras the squared length is ‖e‖² plus something positive. Every other point is farther away than p.

Two quick sanity checks

If b already lies on the line, then e = 0 and p = b: you are already on the road. If b is perpendicular to a, then aᵀb = 0, so x̂ = 0 and p = 0: the closest point is the origin.

Where this is used

A drone flies east and the wind blows at (−3, 4) m/s. The part of the wind along the flight direction is the projection onto (1, 0): a 3 m/s headwind. The rest, 4 m/s, pushes sideways. Flight controllers split wind and forces this way all the time.

Example 1 · Onto the diagonal

Project b = (3, 1) onto the line through a = (1, 1).

aᵀb = 4,   aᵀa = 2,   x̂ = 2
p = (2, 2),   e = (1, −1)
a · e = 1 − 1 = 0 ✓

Example 2 · A line in 3D

Project b = (1, 2, 6) onto the line through a = (1, 1, 1).

aᵀb = 9,   aᵀa = 3,   x̂ = 3
p = (3, 3, 3),   e = (−2, −1, 3)
a · e = −2 − 1 + 3 = 0 ✓

Projecting onto (1, 1, 1) replaced every component by the average, 3. Keep that in mind for least squares.

Example 3 · Headwind and crosswind

A drone heads in direction a = (1, 0). The wind is w = (−3, 4) m/s.

aᵀw = −3,   aᵀa = 1,   along the path: p = (−3, 0)
sideways: w − p = (0, 4)

A 3 m/s headwind (negative, so against the motion) and a 4 m/s crosswind. Their squared sizes add: 9 + 16 = 25 = ‖w‖².

Project b = (2, −1) onto the line through a = (1, 2). Explain the answer.

Show solutionHide solution

aᵀb = 2 − 2 = 0, so x̂ = 0 and p = (0, 0).

b is perpendicular to the line, so the closest point on the line is the origin.

3

Replace a by 2a. Does the projection change?

Show solutionHide solution

No. aᵀb doubles and aᵀa becomes four times larger, so x̂ is halved. Then p = x̂·(2a) is the same vector as before.

This makes sense: 2a describes the same line, and the closest point depends only on the line.

import numpy as np a = np.array([1., 2.]) # the line through a b = np.array([4., 3.]) # the point we project x_hat = 1.0 # your turn: x_hat = (a @ b) / (a @ a) p = x_hat * a e = b - p print("p =", p, " e =", e, " a · e =", a @ e) proj(a, b, p) # right angle at p means you got it
