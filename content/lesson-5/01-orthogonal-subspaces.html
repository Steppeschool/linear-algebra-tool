  2. Then Pythagoras holds: ‖v‖² + ‖w‖² = ‖v + w‖², where ‖v‖ is the length of v.

Perpendicular directions do not interfere

Walk along a street that runs exactly north. However far you go, your east-west position does not change. North and east are perpendicular directions: moving in one tells you nothing about the other.

In algebra the test is the dot product. Multiply matching components and add. If the total is zero, the vectors are perpendicular. For v = (2, 1) and w = (−1, 2): v · w = −2 + 2 = 0.

We also write the dot product as vᵀw: a row times a column. It is the same number.

Why the dot product is the right test

Write ‖v‖ for the length of v. Its square is the dot product of v with itself: ‖v‖² = v · v = vᵀv. For a right angle, Pythagoras says the squared lengths add up. Check it on the example:

‖v‖² = 4 + 1 = 5    ‖w‖² = 1 + 4 = 5
v + w = (1, 3),   ‖v + w‖² = 1 + 9 = 10 = 5 + 5 ✓

In general ‖v + w‖² = ‖v‖² + 2vᵀw + ‖w‖². The middle term vanishes exactly when vᵀw = 0. So "dot product zero" and "right angle" are the same statement.

Perpendicular subspaces

Now raise the level. Two subspaces V and W are orthogonal if every vector in V is perpendicular to every vector in W. The floor of a room and a vertical line through the corner are an example: the line is perpendicular to every direction on the floor.

Look again at Ax = 0. Each component of Ax is one row of A dotted with x. So Ax = 0 says: x is perpendicular to every row. Then x is also perpendicular to every combination of the rows, which is the whole row space C(Aᵀ). The reason is one line: x · (c₁r₁ + c₂r₂) = c₁(x · r₁) + c₂(x · r₂) = 0 + 0.

A = 1326   row space: the line through (1, 3)
nullspace: the line through (−3, 1)
(1, 3) · (−3, 1) = −3 + 3 = 0 ✓

The same reasoning with Aᵀ in place of A shows that the left nullspace N(Aᵀ) is perpendicular to the column space C(A).

The two pieces fill the space

Recall from lesson 4: r is the rank of A (its number of pivots) and n is its number of columns. The row space has dimension r and the nullspace has dimension n − r. Together that is n, the whole input space. And the nullspace contains every vector perpendicular to the row space: such a vector is perpendicular to each row, so it solves Ax = 0. Strang calls the two subspaces orthogonal complements.

This means every input x splits in exactly one way into x = x_r + x_n, a row space part plus a nullspace part. Since Ax_n = 0, the matrix only "sees" the row space part: Ax = Ax_r.

Where this is used

An IMU (the motion sensor in a phone or drone) has three accelerometer axes at right angles. Because the axes are perpendicular, each one measures its own direction only. Tilt the board along x, and the y and z readings stay put, apart from noise.

Example 1 · Test with the dot product

Are v = (1, 2, 3) and w = (3, 0, −1) perpendicular?

v · w = 1·3 + 2·0 + 3·(−1) = 3 + 0 − 3 = 0 ✓
Pythagoras: ‖v‖² = 14, ‖w‖² = 10, v + w = (4, 2, 2), ‖v + w‖² = 24 = 14 + 10 ✓

Yes. In 3D the test is the same: one product per component, then add.

Example 2 · Rows against the nullspace

A = 121242. Rank 1, so the row space is the line through (1, 2, 1).

Special solutions of Ax = 0: s₁ = (−2, 1, 0), s₂ = (−1, 0, 1)
(1, 2, 1) · (−2, 1, 0) = −2 + 2 + 0 = 0 ✓
(1, 2, 1) · (−1, 0, 1) = −1 + 0 + 1 = 0 ✓

The nullspace is a plane, the row space is a line, and the line sticks straight out of the plane. Dimensions 2 + 1 = 3.

Can the row space and the nullspace of a 3 by 3 matrix both be planes?

Show solutionHide solution

No. Their dimensions are r and 3 − r, which add to 3, not 4.

There is also a picture reason: two planes through the origin in 3D always share a line. A vector on that line would have to be perpendicular to itself, and only the zero vector is.
