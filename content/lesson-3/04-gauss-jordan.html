Use row operations to turn A into I. The same operations turn I into A⁻¹. [ A  |  I ]  →  [ I  |  A⁻¹ ]

Think: carbon paper

Put a sheet of carbon paper under your work. Every row operation you do on A gets copied onto the identity beside it.

When A has been turned into I, the copy holds the record of everything you did. Undoing A is exactly what that record does, so the copy is A⁻¹.

The steps

  • Forward: clear below the pivots, as in lesson 2.
  • Backward: clear above the pivots too, from the bottom up.
  • Scale: divide each row by its pivot so the diagonal becomes 1s.

The result is the reduced row echelon form. The lab's rref does all three steps.

Worked example

K = 2−10−12−10−12 shows up for a chain of springs and masses.
K⁻¹ = ¼ · 321242123
Check one column: K times (3, 2, 1) = (6 − 2, −3 + 4 − 1, −2 + 2) = (4, 0, 0), which is 4 times the first column of I ✓

Don't use it to solve Ax = b

Computing A⁻¹ costs about three times the work of elimination, and it adds rounding error. To solve Ax = b, use elimination (A = LU). As Strang stresses: the inverse is great for thinking, but don't compute A⁻¹ to solve Ax = b.

Example 1 · A 2 by 2 by hand

Invert A = 2111.

[2 1 | 1 0; 1 1 | 0 1] → row 2 − ½·row 1 → [2 1 | 1 0; 0 ½ | −½ 1]
row 2 × 2 → [0 1 | −1 2]; row 1 − row 2 → [2 0 | 2 −2]; row 1 ÷ 2
A⁻¹ = 1−1−12

Example 2 · A triangular matrix

Invert A = 120013001. Only upward steps are needed.

row 2 − 3·row 3, then row 1 − 2·row 2
A⁻¹ = 1−2601−3001

The 6 comes from the two steps interacting: −2 · (−3).

Example 3 · When Gauss-Jordan stops

Try A = 1224.

row 2 − 2·row 1 → [1 2 | 1 0; 0 0 | −2 1]

The left side has a zero row, so it can never become I. No inverse.
