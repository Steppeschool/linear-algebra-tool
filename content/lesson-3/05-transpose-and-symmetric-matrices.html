A matrix that equals its own transpose is called symmetric, and the product AᵀA is always symmetric. 123456ᵀ = 135246
  1. Row i of A becomes column i of Aᵀ. An m by n matrix becomes n by m.
  2. (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ: the order reverses, as for inverses.
  3. Symmetric means S = Sᵀ: entry (i, j) equals entry (j, i).
  4. AᵀA is always symmetric. It is the heart of least squares in the next part of the course (Strang, chapter 4).

Think of turning a spreadsheet

A sales sheet has one row per product and one column per month. Your manager asks for months as rows instead. No number changes; only the way you read them does. That is the transpose.

123456ᵀ = 135246

Symmetric matrices: the distance table

A road atlas has a table of distances between cities. The distance from Paris to Lyon equals the distance from Lyon to Paris, so if you flip the table across its diagonal, nothing changes. Such a table is a symmetric matrix.

The rules

  • (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ. The order reverses, just as for inverses on slide 3.
  • (A⁻¹)ᵀ = (Aᵀ)⁻¹. Transposing and inverting can be done in either order.
  • The dot product of x and y can be written xᵀy: a row times a column.
  • AᵀA is symmetric. Its (i, j) entry is column i of A dotted with column j. Swapping i and j gives the same dot product.

A worked example

A = 123456 is 3 by 2, so AᵀA is 2 by 2.
(1, 1): 1 + 9 + 25 = 35   (1, 2): 2 + 12 + 30 = 44   (2, 2): 4 + 16 + 36 = 56
AᵀA = 35444456

Where this is used

AᵀA appears whenever we fit a model to many noisy measurements, for example a straight line through sensor readings or the calibration of an accelerometer. For a rotation matrix Q, QᵀQ = I, so the transpose is the inverse: turning back costs nothing to compute.
