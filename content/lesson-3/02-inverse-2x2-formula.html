  1. A⁻¹ = 1/(ad − bc) times [[d, −b], [−c, a]].
  2. Dividing by det A undoes the area scaling of A.
  3. If ad − bc = 0 there is no inverse.
  4. Always check the answer by multiplying: AA⁻¹ should be I.

The recipe

abcd⁻¹ = 1/(ad − bc) · d−b−ca

Three moves: swap a and d, flip the signs of b and c, divide everything by ad − bc.

Why the determinant appears

Lesson 1 showed that A multiplies every area by |det A|, where det A = ad − bc, and that the sign of det A records whether the plane is flipped. To undo A, its inverse must shrink areas back by the same factor, so it must divide by det A.

When det A = 0, A squashes the plane onto a line. Undoing that would mean dividing by zero, and no matrix can turn a line back into a plane. So a matrix with ad − bc = 0 has no inverse.

A worked example

A = 2153   det A = 2·3 − 1·5 = 1
A⁻¹ = 1/1 · 3−1−52
Check: AA⁻¹ = 6 − 5−2 + 215 − 15−5 + 6 = 1001 ✓

Common mistakes

  • Swapping b and c instead of a and d. Only the diagonal entries trade places.
  • Flipping the signs of a and d. Only b and c change sign.
  • Forgetting to divide by det A. The example above hides this because det A = 1.

Bigger matrices

There is a formula for larger matrices too (chapter 5), but it is far too slow to use. In practice the inverse comes from elimination, as slide 4 shows.

Example 1 · Determinant 1

A = 2312, det A = 4 − 3 = 1.

A⁻¹ = 2−3−12

Example 2 · Determinant −2

A = 1234, det A = 4 − 6 = −2.

A⁻¹ = −½ · 4−2−31 = −211.5−0.5

Example 3 · No inverse

A = 2412, det A = 4 − 4 = 0.
