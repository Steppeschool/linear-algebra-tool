Now we find it. For a 2 by 2 matrix there is a short recipe with three moves. Swap the two numbers on the diagonal, put a minus sign on the other two, and divide everything by one number, ad − bc. That number is called the determinant. Think of the light on a phone charger: before you start, one glance tells you whether it will work. Here the glance is ad − bc. If it is not 0, the recipe gives A⁻¹. If it is 0, the recipe would divide by zero, and A has no inverse at all. abcd⁻¹ = 1/(ad − bc) · d−b−ca
  1. A⁻¹ = 1/(ad − bc) times d−b−ca: swap a and d, flip the signs of b and c, divide by ad − bc.
  2. The number ad − bc is the determinant of A, written det A. For now it means one thing: it says whether a 2 by 2 inverse exists.
  3. A times d−b−ca is (ad − bc)I, so dividing by ad − bc leaves I.
  4. If ad − bc = 0 there is no inverse: the columns lie on one line.
  5. Always check the answer by multiplying: AA⁻¹ should be I.

The recipe

abcd⁻¹ = 1/(ad − bc) · d−b−ca

Swap a and d. Flip the signs of b and c. Divide everything by ad − bc. Here are the three moves on A = 1234, where ad − bc = 1·4 − 2·3 = −2:

swap the diagonal: 4231  →  flip the other signs: 4−2−31  →  divide by −2: −211.5−0.5

Why it works

Multiply A by the swapped-and-flipped matrix, before dividing:

abcdd−b−ca = ad − bc−ab + bacd − dc−cb + da = ad − bc00ad − bc
= (ad − bc) I

The off-diagonal entries cancel. Both diagonal entries are ad − bc. So the product is ad − bc times I. Divide by ad − bc and you get I. With numbers, for A = 1234:

12344−2−31 = 4 − 6−2 + 212 − 12−6 + 4 = −200−2 = −2·I

The determinant: one number that says yes or no

The number ad − bc is called the determinant of A, written det A. In this lesson it has one job: it tells you whether a 2 by 2 matrix has an inverse. If ad − bc ≠ 0, the recipe works. If ad − bc = 0, the recipe would divide by zero, and there is no inverse.

2153: 2·3 − 1·5 = 1  →  inverse exists    2412: 2·2 − 4·1 = 0  →  no inverse

What does ad − bc = 0 look like? Then ad = bc, and the columns (a, c) and (b, d) lie on one line: one is a multiple of the other. For 2412, column 2 = 2·column 1. That is the dependent case from slide 1: two different inputs give the same output, so nothing can undo A.

The rows tell the same story. As equations, 2x + 4y = 4 and x + 2y = 1 have lines with the same slope. They never cross, so this system has no solution at all:

The determinant has much more to say. Lesson 6 shows what it measures in pictures. For now, remember it as the yes-or-no number for a 2 by 2 inverse.

Common mistakes

  • Swapping b and c. Only the diagonal entries, a and d, trade places.
  • Flipping the signs of a and d. Only b and c change sign.
  • Forgetting to divide by det A. The worked example hides this, since det A = 1. The first example, with det A = −2, does not.

A bigger example: a 3 × 3 built from a 2 × 2

The recipe is only for 2 by 2. But it still helps when a 3 by 3 matrix has a 2 by 2 block that does not mix with the rest:

B = 210530004    B⁻¹ = 3−10−52000¼
Row 1 of BB⁻¹: 2·3 + 1·(−5)2·(−1) + 1·20 = 100
Row 2: 5·3 + 3·(−5)5·(−1) + 3·20 = 010   Row 3: 004·¼ = 001 ✓

The top-left corner is the A from the worked example, so its inverse goes in the same corner, and the 4 is undone by ¼. For a general 3 by 3, or a 1000 by 1000, there is no short recipe worth using: the inverse comes from elimination, on slide 4.
