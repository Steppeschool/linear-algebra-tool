A few special ones do not: A only stretches them (or shrinks or flips them). These are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue. They show what a matrix really does. Ax = λx    (x ≠ 0)
  1. An eigenvector of A is a nonzero vector x whose direction A keeps: Ax = λx.
  2. The number λ (lambda) is its eigenvalue: the stretch factor along that direction.
  3. λ > 1 stretches, 0 < λ < 1 shrinks, λ < 0 flips the arrow, λ = 0 squashes it to 0.
  4. Any multiple of an eigenvector is again an eigenvector, with the same λ. What matters is the line.

Pulling a rubber sheet

Draw arrows in every direction on a rubber sheet, then pull the sheet along one diagonal. Most arrows turn as the sheet deforms. But the arrows that lie exactly along the pull stay on their line: they only get longer. And the arrows exactly across the pull also stay on their line: they keep their length or shrink, but they do not turn.

Those unturned directions are the eigenvectors of the stretch. How much each one grows is its eigenvalue.

The equation

"A keeps the direction of x" means Ax is a multiple of x. Call the multiple λ (the Greek letter lambda):

Ax = λx,   with x ≠ 0

The zero vector always satisfies A0 = λ0, so it does not count. An eigenvector must be nonzero.

A worked example

A = 2112
x = (1, 1):   Ax = (2 + 1, 1 + 2) = (3, 3) = 3·(1, 1).   Eigenvector, λ = 3
x = (1, −1):   Ax = (2 − 1, 1 − 2) = (1, −1) = 1·(1, −1).   Eigenvector, λ = 1
x = (1, 0):   Ax = (2, 1). Not a multiple of (1, 0): A turned it

So this A stretches the diagonal direction (1, 1) by 3 and leaves the direction (1, −1) alone. Any other vector is a sum of these two. A stretches the two parts by different amounts, so the sum turns.

Why they matter

Multiply by A again and again and the eigenvectors keep their direction: A²x = λ²x, A¹⁰x = λ¹⁰x. That makes them the key to anything that repeats: populations year after year, a filter updating every millisecond, a vibrating structure. The next slides find eigenvalues by calculation and use them.

Where this is used

A spinning object such as a drone or satellite has three special axes, its principal axes. Spun about one of them, it rotates smoothly; spun about any other axis, it wobbles. Those axes are the eigenvectors of its inertia matrix (a matrix that describes how its mass is spread out).

Example 1 · A stretch along the axes

A = 300½.

A(1, 0) = (3, 0) = 3·(1, 0):   λ = 3
A(0, 1) = (0, ½) = ½·(0, 1):   λ = ½

For a diagonal matrix the eigenvectors are the axes and the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries.

Example 2 · A mirror

The mirror across the line y = x, A = 0110.

A(1, 1) = (1, 1):   λ = 1 (the mirror line stays put)
A(1, −1) = (−1, 1) = −1·(1, −1):   λ = −1 (flipped)

Example 3 · A rotation has none

The quarter turn A = 0−110 turns every arrow by 90°.

No real direction stays on its line, so there is no real eigenvector. (The eigenvalues turn out to be complex numbers, i and −i.)
