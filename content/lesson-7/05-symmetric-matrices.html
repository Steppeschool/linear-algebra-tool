All their eigenvalues are real numbers. And we can always pick eigenvectors that meet at right angles, then shrink or stretch them to length 1. Then X becomes an orthogonal matrix Q, its inverse is just Qᵀ, and A = QΛQᵀ. A = Aᵀ ⇒ A = QΛQᵀ    QᵀQ = I
  1. Every eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix is a real number (never complex, unlike the rotation on slide 1).
  2. Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are perpendicular.
  3. Scaled to length 1, they form the columns of an orthogonal Q (lesson 5): Q⁻¹ = Qᵀ.
  4. So A = QΛQᵀ. (This result is called the spectral theorem.) Every symmetric matrix is a stretch along perpendicular axes.

Pulling a sheet in two perpendicular directions

Stretch a sheet of rubber harder east-west than north-south. The two stretch directions are perpendicular, and each is a direction the stretch does not turn. That is what a symmetric matrix always looks like: stretches (or squeezes, or flips) along perpendicular axes, and nothing else.

Why the eigenvectors are perpendicular

Take Ax = λ₁x and Ay = λ₂y with λ₁ ≠ λ₂. Compute xᵀAy in two ways:

xᵀ(Ay) = λ₂ xᵀy
(Ax)ᵀy = λ₁ xᵀy,   and (Ax)ᵀy = xᵀAᵀy = xᵀAy because A = Aᵀ
So λ₁ xᵀy = λ₂ xᵀy. Since λ₁ ≠ λ₂, xᵀy = 0: x ⟂ y

A worked example

A = 3113: trace 6, det 8, λ² − 6λ + 8 = (λ − 4)(λ − 2)
λ = 4 with (1, 1),   λ = 2 with (1, −1).   (1, 1) · (1, −1) = 0 ✓
Unit length: q₁ = (1, 1)/√2,   q₂ = (1, −1)/√2
Q = (1/√2)111−1,   Λ = 4002,   A = QΛQᵀ

Forget the √2 and use Q = [1 1; 1 −1]: then QᵀQ = 2I, not I, and QΛQᵀ comes out as 2A. Length 1 matters. That is the lab exercise.

A sum of simple pieces

Multiplying out QΛQᵀ gives A = λ₁q₁q₁ᵀ + λ₂q₂q₂ᵀ. Each qqᵀ is a projection onto one axis, because q has length 1 (lesson 5). So A splits x onto the two axes, stretches each piece by its λ, and adds the pieces back. Here A = 4·(projection onto (1, 1)) + 2·(projection onto (1, −1)).

Where this is used

Covariance matrices of sensor data are symmetric. Their perpendicular eigenvectors are the directions in which the data varies most and least. That is principal component analysis (PCA), which comes with the SVD in the next lesson. The inertia matrix of a drone is symmetric too: its eigenvectors are the principal axes from slide 1.

Example 1 · A diagonal matrix

A = 5001: Q = I, Λ = A. The axes are already perpendicular eigenvectors.

Example 2 · Negative eigenvalues are fine

A = 2332: λ = 5 with (1, 1), λ = −1 with (1, −1).

Still perpendicular: (1, 1) · (1, −1) = 0
A stretches by 5 along (1, 1) and flips along (1, −1)

Example 3 · A non-symmetric contrast

A = 4123 (slide 2) has eigenvectors (1, 1) and (1, −2).

(1, 1) · (1, −2) = −1 ≠ 0

Not perpendicular: without symmetry there is no such guarantee.

Find Q and Λ for 5222 (eigenvalues 6 and 1).

Show solutionHide solution

λ = 6: A − 6I = [−1 2; 2 −4], x = (2, 1). λ = 1: A − I = [4 2; 2 1], x = (1, −2). Perpendicular ✓. Q = (1/√5)[2 1; 1 −2], Λ = diag(6, 1).

2

Show that AᵀA is always symmetric, so its eigenvectors can be chosen perpendicular.

Show solutionHide solution

(AᵀA)ᵀ = Aᵀ(Aᵀ)ᵀ = AᵀA. This fact is the starting point of the SVD.

3

A symmetric 2 by 2 matrix with two different eigenvalues has eigenvector (3, 4). What is the other eigenvector direction?

Show solutionHide solution

It must be perpendicular: (4, −3) or any multiple.

import numpy as np A = np.array([[3., 1.], [1., 3.]]) Q = np.array([[1., 1.], [1., -1.]]) # eigenvectors (1, 1) and (1, -1) as columns # your turn: divide by their length, sqrt(2) Lam = np.array([[4., 0.], [0., 2.]]) print("Q.T @ Q =") print(Q.T @ Q) # orthonormal columns give the identity table(("A", A), ("Q", Q), ("Q Lam Q.T", Q @ Lam @ Q.T))
