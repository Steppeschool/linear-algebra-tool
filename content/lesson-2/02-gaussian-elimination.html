That is fine for two lines, but nobody can look at a hundred. Elimination is a recipe that does the work step by step. You already know it from the café. A coffee and a croissant cost €5, and a coffee and two croissants cost €7. The second order has one more croissant and costs €2 more, so a croissant is €2 and the coffee is €3. You subtracted one order from the other and the coffee disappeared. Elimination does exactly that: subtract a multiple of one equation from another so that one unknown disappears, then solve what is left. x − 2y = 1    3x + 2y = 11   →   8y = 8
  1. The pivot is the first coefficient (the number in front of an unknown) of the equation we subtract with (equation 1; here the 1 in 1x). We use it to remove x from the equations below.
  2. The multiplier is ℓ = (entry to remove) ÷ (pivot). In code we write ℓ as l.
  3. Subtracting ℓ times one equation from another keeps the same solution.
  4. When one unknown is left in the last equation, we solve it and work back up: back substitution.

The café puzzle as equations

Call the price of a coffee x and the price of a croissant y. The two orders say:

x + y = 5    x + 2y = 7
(x + 2y) − (x + y) = 7 − 5  →  y = 2
Back to the first order: x + 2 = 5, so x = 3

One subtraction, and x was gone. Every system is solved this way. We only need a rule for how much to subtract.

The same idea, step by step

Take Strang's first system from slide 1: x − 2y = 1 and 3x + 2y = 11.

  • Equation 1 starts with 1x. That 1 is the pivot. It clears the x below.
  • Equation 2 has 3x, so we need 3 copies of equation 1. That 3 is the multiplier, ℓ = 3 ÷ 1 = 3.
  • Subtract 3 times equation 1 from equation 2. The x terms cancel.
(3x + 2y) − 3(x − 2y) = 11 − 3·1
8y = 8,  so y = 1
Back up to equation 1: x − 2·1 = 1,  so x = 3

Putting y = 1 back into equation 1 is called back substitution.

What it looks like

Draw the lines before and after the step. Line 1 stays where it is. Line 2 turns until it is horizontal: 8y = 8 is the line y = 1. The crossing point (3, 1) never moves, because the step did not change the solution.

Why the answer does not change

Any (x, y) that solves both equations also solves "equation 2 minus 3 times equation 1". And adding 3 times equation 1 back undoes the step. So the new system has exactly the same solutions, just easier to read.

Only the numbers matter

The letters x and y only hold the numbers in place. So we can drop them and keep a table. Each equation becomes one row: the number in front of x, the number in front of y, a bar, and the right side. This table is called the augmented matrix [A | b]. The step "row 2 − 3·row 1" acts on the whole row, the right side too.

[A | b] = 1−2|132|11  →  1−2|108|8

A bigger example: 3 equations

Slide 1's bigger system works the same way. First use equation 1 to remove x from equations 2 and 3. Then use the new equation 2 to remove y from equation 3.

x + y + z = 6    2x + y − z = 1    x + 2y + z = 8
equation 2 − 2·equation 1:  −y − 3z = −11
equation 3 − 1·equation 1:  y = 2
(Lucky: this already gives y. We don't rely on luck, so we carry on with the recipe.)
new equation 3 + new equation 2:  −3z = −9, so z = 3
Back up: −y − 9 = −11, so y = 2 (as we saw). Then x + 2 + 3 = 6, so x = 1

The answer x = 1, y = 2, z = 3 matches slide 1. Circuit simulators and bridge-design programs follow the same steps with thousands of equations. The computer just does the arithmetic.

Next: With three or more equations, the order of the steps matters. Slide 3 clears one column at a time, names the pivots, and climbs back up with back substitution.

Example 1 · Subtract twice the first equation

Solve 2x + y = 5 and 4x − y = 1.

Pivot 2, multiplier ℓ = 4 ÷ 2 = 2
(4x − y) − 2(2x + y) = 1 − 2·5  →  −3y = −9  →  y = 3
Back up: 2x + 3 = 5, so x = 1
Check: 4·1 − 3 = 1 ✓

Example 2 · A pivot of 1

Solve x + 3y = 7 and 2x + y = 4.

ℓ = 2 ÷ 1 = 2.   (2x + y) − 2(x + 3y) = 4 − 14  →  −5y = −10  →  y = 2
x + 6 = 7, so x = 1

A pivot of 1 is the friendliest case: the multiplier is just the entry below it.

Example 3 · A fractional multiplier

Solve 3x + 2y = 7 and x + 4y = 9.

ℓ = 1 ÷ 3 = 1/3
(x + 4y) − (1/3)(3x + 2y) = 9 − 7/3  →  (10/3) y = 20/3  →  y = 2
3x + 4 = 7, so x = 1

Fractions are fine. The rule never changes: multiplier = entry to remove ÷ pivot.
