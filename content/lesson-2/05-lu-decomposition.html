Think: meal prep

On Sunday you chop every vegetable for the week. That is the slow part. On weekdays each dinner takes five minutes.

Factoring A = LU is the Sunday work. Each new right-hand side b is a weekday dinner: two quick triangular solves.

Lc = b  (forward, top to bottom)    then    Ux = c  (back, bottom to top)

Why the multipliers drop right in

Elimination is E₃₂E₃₁E₂₁A = U. Undo the cards in reverse order and you get A = E₂₁⁻¹E₃₁⁻¹E₃₂⁻¹U. That product of inverses is L.

Each inverse just has +ℓ where E had −ℓ. Multiplied in this order, they don't interfere: every ℓ lands in its own position. No extra arithmetic. L is the receipt of every step you took.

Worked example

From the last two slides: ℓ₂₁ = 2, ℓ₃₁ = −1, ℓ₃₂ = −1.
L = 100210−1−11   U = 2110−8−2001
Row 3 of LU: −1·(2, 1, 1) − 1·(0, −8, −2) + 1·(0, 0, 1) = (−2, 7, 2) ✓

Why it matters

For n unknowns, factoring costs about n³/3 operations. Each solve after that costs about n². With n = 1000: around 330 million for the factoring, then about a million per new b.

So when the matrix stays the same and only the right side changes, as in a simulation stepping through time, you factor once and reuse it. The lab has lu(A) if you want to check your own matrices.

import numpy as np U = np.array([[2., 1., 1.], [0., -8., -2.], [0., 0., 1.]]) L = np.array([[ 1., 0., 0.], [ 2., 1., 0.], [-1., 0., 1.]]) # one multiplier is missing table(("L", L), ("U", U), ("L @ U", L @ U))
