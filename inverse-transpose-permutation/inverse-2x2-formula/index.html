Strang §2.5 · The 2 by 2 inverse

Swap, flip signs, divide by the determinant.

For a 2 by 2 matrix the inverse has a short formula: swap the two diagonal entries, change the signs of the other two, and divide by the determinant ad − bc.

Lesson 3 · slide 22 × 2 inverse
abcd⁻¹ = 1/(ad − bc) · d−b−ca
Key ideas
  1. A⁻¹ = 1/(ad − bc) times [[d, −b], [−c, a]].
  2. A times [[d, −b], [−c, a]] is (ad − bc)I, so dividing by ad − bc leaves I.
  3. If ad − bc = 0 there is no inverse.
  4. Always check the answer by multiplying: AA⁻¹ should be I.

The recipe

abcd⁻¹ = 1/(ad − bc) · d−b−ca

Three moves: swap a and d, flip the signs of b and c, divide everything by ad − bc.

Why the recipe works

Multiply A by the swapped-and-flipped matrix, before any division:

abcdd−b−ca = ad − bc−ab + bacd − dc−cb + da = ad − bc00ad − bc
= (ad − bc) I

The off-diagonal entries cancel, and both diagonal entries are ad − bc. So the product is ad − bc times I. Divide by ad − bc and you get exactly I. That is the whole formula.

Why the determinant appears

Lesson 1 showed that A multiplies every area by |det A|, where det A = ad − bc. The swapped-and-flipped matrix d−b−ca has determinant da − (−b)(−c) = ad − bc too. So, as shown above, A times it is (ad − bc)I, not I. Dividing by ad − bc removes that extra factor and leaves I.

When det A = 0, A squashes the plane onto a line. Undoing that would mean dividing by zero, and no matrix can turn a line back into a plane. So a matrix with ad − bc = 0 has no inverse.

A worked example

A = 2153   det A = 2·3 − 1·5 = 1
A⁻¹ = 1/1 · 3−1−52
Check: AA⁻¹ = 6 − 5−2 + 215 − 15−5 + 6 = 1001 ✓

Common mistakes

  • Swapping b and c instead of a and d. Only the diagonal entries trade places.
  • Flipping the signs of a and d. Only b and c change sign.
  • Forgetting to divide by det A. The example above hides this because det A = 1.

Bigger matrices

There is a formula for larger matrices too (Strang, chapter 5), but it is far too slow to use. In practice the inverse comes from elimination, as slide 4 shows.

Worked examples

Example 1 · Determinant 1

A = 2312, det A = 4 − 3 = 1.

A⁻¹ = 2−3−12

Example 2 · Determinant −2

A = 1234, det A = 4 − 6 = −2.

A⁻¹ = −½ · 4−2−31 = −211.5−0.5

Example 3 · No inverse

A = 2412, det A = 4 − 4 = 0.

The formula would divide by 0. The columns are dependent, and A has no inverse

Worked examples

Example 1 · A 2 by 2 inverse

Find the inverse of A = 4726.

det A = 4·6 − 7·2 = 10
A⁻¹ = 1/10 · 6−7−24
Check, top-left entry of AA⁻¹: (4·6 + 7·(−2)) / 10 = 1 ✓

Example 2 · Gauss-Jordan step by step

Invert A = 1237 by elimination on [A | I].

[1 2 | 1 0; 3 7 | 0 1]  → row 2 − 3·row 1 →  [1 2 | 1 0; 0 1 | −3 1]
row 1 − 2·row 2 →  [1 0 | 7 −2; 0 1 | −3 1]
A⁻¹ = 7−2−31

det A = 1, so the formula gives the same answer with no fractions.

Example 3 · The transpose of a product

Check (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ for A = 1201 and B = 1031.

AB = 7231, so (AB)ᵀ = 7321
BᵀAᵀ = 13011021 = 7321 ✓

AᵀBᵀ would be 1327: the wrong order gives a different matrix.

Show solutionHide solution

B⁻¹ = ½00⅓ and A⁻¹ = 1−101.

B⁻¹A⁻¹ = ½−½0⅓. Check: AB = 2303 and its inverse from the formula, 1/6·3−302, is the same ✓

4

Compute AᵀA for A = 102103. Is it symmetric?

Show solutionHide solution

AᵀA = 52210. Entry (1, 1) = 1 + 4 + 0, entry (1, 2) = 0 + 2 + 0, entry (2, 2) = 0 + 1 + 9.

Yes, it is symmetric, as AᵀA always is.

5

Find the permutation P that puts the rows of a 3 by 3 matrix in the order 2, 3, 1. What is P⁻¹?

Show solutionHide solution

The rows of P are rows 2, 3, 1 of I: P = 010001100.

P⁻¹ = Pᵀ = 001100010, which puts the rows back.
