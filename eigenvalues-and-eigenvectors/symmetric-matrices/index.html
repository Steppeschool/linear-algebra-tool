Strang §6.4 · Symmetric matrices

Symmetric matrices: perpendicular eigenvectors.

Symmetric matrices (A = Aᵀ) are the friendliest case. All their eigenvalues are real numbers. And we can always pick eigenvectors that meet at right angles, then shrink or stretch them to length 1. Then X becomes an orthogonal matrix Q, its inverse is just Qᵀ, and A = QΛQᵀ.

Lesson 7 · slide 5Symmetric matrices
A = Aᵀ ⇒ A = QΛQᵀ    QᵀQ = I
Key ideas
  1. Every eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix is a real number (never complex, unlike the rotation on slide 1).
  2. Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are perpendicular.
  3. Scaled to length 1, they form the columns of an orthogonal Q (lesson 5): Q⁻¹ = Qᵀ.
  4. So A = QΛQᵀ. (This result is called the spectral theorem.) Every symmetric matrix is a stretch along perpendicular axes.

Pulling a sheet in two perpendicular directions

Stretch a sheet of rubber harder east-west than north-south. The two stretch directions are perpendicular, and each is a direction the stretch does not turn. That is what a symmetric matrix always looks like: stretches (or squeezes, or flips) along perpendicular axes, and nothing else.

Why the eigenvectors are perpendicular

Take Ax = λ₁x and Ay = λ₂y with λ₁ ≠ λ₂. Compute xᵀAy in two ways:

xᵀ(Ay) = λ₂ xᵀy
(Ax)ᵀy = λ₁ xᵀy,   and (Ax)ᵀy = xᵀAᵀy = xᵀAy because A = Aᵀ
So λ₁ xᵀy = λ₂ xᵀy. Since λ₁ ≠ λ₂, xᵀy = 0: x ⟂ y

A worked example

A = 3113: trace 6, det 8, λ² − 6λ + 8 = (λ − 4)(λ − 2)
λ = 4 with (1, 1),   λ = 2 with (1, −1).   (1, 1) · (1, −1) = 0 ✓
Unit length: q₁ = (1, 1)/√2,   q₂ = (1, −1)/√2
Q = (1/√2)111−1,   Λ = 4002,   A = QΛQᵀ

Forget the √2 and use Q = [1 1; 1 −1]: then QᵀQ = 2I, not I, and QΛQᵀ comes out as 2A. Length 1 matters. That is the lab exercise.
