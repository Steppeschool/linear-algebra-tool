Strang §6.4 · Symmetric matrices

Symmetric matrices: perpendicular eigenvectors

On slides 2 to 4 the eigenvectors could point any way: 11 and 1−2 meet at a slant. Symmetric matrices (A = Aᵀ, from lesson 3) are much tidier. Think of pizza dough pulled harder east–west than north–south. The two pull directions don't turn, and they are at a right angle. Every symmetric matrix acts like that: it stretches or flips along perpendicular axes, and does nothing else. Its eigenvalues are real numbers, and its eigenvectors can be chosen perpendicular and of length 1. Put those unit eigenvectors in the columns of Q. Then Q is an orthogonal matrix (lesson 5), its inverse is simply Qᵀ, and A = QΛQᵀ. This fact is called the spectral theorem.

Lesson 7 · slide 5Symmetric matrices
A = Aᵀ ⇒ A = QΛQᵀ    QᵀQ = I
Key ideas
  1. Every eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix is a real number (never complex, unlike the rotation on slide 1).
  2. Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are perpendicular.
  3. Scaled to length 1, they form the columns of an orthogonal Q (lesson 5): Q⁻¹ = Qᵀ.
  4. So A = QΛQᵀ. (This result is called the spectral theorem.) Every symmetric matrix is a stretch along perpendicular axes.

Stretching along perpendicular axes

Take the symmetric A = 3113: row 1 and column 1 hold the same numbers. Trace 6, det 9 − 1 = 8:

λ² − 6λ + 8 = (λ − 4)(λ − 2) = 0
λ = 4 with 11,   λ = 2 with 1−1.   11 · 1−1 = 1 − 1 = 0 ✓ perpendicular

A circle shows the same thing. The unit circle becomes an ellipse whose long and short axes lie exactly on the eigenvector lines. The long half-axis is 4, the short one is 2.

Why the eigenvectors are perpendicular

Take Ax = λ₁x and Ay = λ₂y with λ₁ ≠ λ₂. Compute xᵀAy two ways (the transpose rules are from lesson 3):

xᵀ(Ay) = λ₂ xᵀy
(Ax)ᵀy = λ₁ xᵀy,   and (Ax)ᵀy = xᵀAᵀy = xᵀAy because A = Aᵀ
So λ₁ xᵀy = λ₂ xᵀy. Since λ₁ ≠ λ₂, xᵀy = 0: x ⟂ y

Without symmetry the second line fails, and so does the right angle. The matrix 4123 from slide 2 is not symmetric, and its eigenvectors give 11 · 1−2 = −1 ≠ 0.

Unit length: from X to Q

Divide each eigenvector by its length, √2 here, to get unit vectors. They are perpendicular and of length 1, so the matrix Q with them as columns has QᵀQ = I (lesson 5):

q₁ = 11/√2,   q₂ = 1−1/√2
Q = (1/√2)111−1,   Λ = 4002,   A = QΛQᵀ
QᵀQ = ½1 + 11 − 11 − 11 + 1 = 1001 ✓

Length 1 matters. Without the √2, QᵀQ = 2I and QΛQᵀ comes out as 2A. That is the lab exercise.

A sum of simple pieces

Multiplied out, A = λ₁q₁q₁ᵀ + λ₂q₂q₂ᵀ. Each qqᵀ is the projection matrix onto one axis (lesson 5). So A splits x onto the axes, stretches each piece by its λ, and adds them back:

4·½1111 + 2·½1−1−11 = 2222 + 1−1−11 = 3113 ✓
