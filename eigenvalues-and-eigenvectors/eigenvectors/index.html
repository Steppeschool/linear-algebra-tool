Strang §6.1 · Introduction to eigenvalues

Eigenvectors: the directions A does not turn.

Feed a vector into a matrix and it usually comes out pointing somewhere new. A few special ones do not: A only stretches them (or shrinks or flips them). These are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue. They show what a matrix really does.

Lesson 7 · slide 1Eigenvectors
Ax = λx    (x ≠ 0)
Key ideas
  1. An eigenvector of A is a nonzero vector x whose direction A keeps: Ax = λx.
  2. The number λ (lambda) is its eigenvalue: the stretch factor along that direction.
  3. λ > 1 stretches, 0 < λ < 1 shrinks, λ < 0 flips the arrow, λ = 0 squashes it to 0.
  4. Any multiple of an eigenvector is again an eigenvector, with the same λ. What matters is the line.

Pulling a rubber sheet

Draw arrows in every direction on a rubber sheet, then pull the sheet along one diagonal. Most arrows turn as the sheet deforms. But the arrows that lie exactly along the pull stay on their line: they only get longer. And the arrows exactly across the pull also stay on their line: they keep their length or shrink, but they do not turn.

Those unturned directions are the eigenvectors of the stretch. How much each one grows is its eigenvalue.

The equation

"A keeps the direction of x" means Ax is a multiple of x. Call the multiple λ (the Greek letter lambda):

Ax = λx,   with x ≠ 0

The zero vector always satisfies A0 = λ0, so it does not count. An eigenvector must be nonzero.

A worked example

A = 2112
x = (1, 1):   Ax = (2 + 1, 1 + 2) = (3, 3) = 3·(1, 1).   Eigenvector, λ = 3
x = (1, −1):   Ax = (2 − 1, 1 − 2) = (1, −1) = 1·(1, −1).   Eigenvector, λ = 1
x = (1, 0):   Ax = (2, 1). Not a multiple of (1, 0): A turned it

So this A stretches the diagonal direction (1, 1) by 3 and leaves the direction (1, −1) alone. Any other vector is a sum of these two. A stretches the two parts by different amounts, so the sum turns.
