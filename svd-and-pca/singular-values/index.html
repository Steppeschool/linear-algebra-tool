Strang §7.2 · Bases and matrices in the SVD

Finding singular values from AᵀA

Slide 1 found the long and short directions of the ellipse by guessing. Now we find them with a recipe. Think of a tailor measuring a sleeve: you don't need to guess the longest stretch of a piece of cloth if you have a tape measure that reads it for you. Here the tape measure is the matrix AᵀA. It is symmetric, so lesson 7 says its eigenvectors are perpendicular. Those eigenvectors are exactly the input directions v₁, v₂. Its eigenvalues are the squared stretches σ₁², σ₂². Take square roots and you have the singular values. Then each output direction is u = Av / σ.

Lesson 8 · slide 2Singular values
AᵀA vᵢ = σᵢ² vᵢ    σᵢ = √λᵢ(AᵀA)    uᵢ = Avᵢ / σᵢ
Key ideas
  1. AᵀA = VΣᵀΣVᵀ: its eigenvectors are the vᵢ and its eigenvalues are σᵢ².
  2. So σᵢ = √(eigenvalue of AᵀA). These eigenvalues are never negative.
  3. The output directions are uᵢ = Avᵢ/σᵢ, automatically perpendicular and of length 1.
  4. Similarly AAᵀ has eigenvectors uᵢ and the same nonzero eigenvalues σᵢ².

Measure the stretch, then take the root

How much does A stretch a unit vector v? Its squared length is a dot product of Av with itself:

|Av|² = (Av)ᵀ(Av) = vᵀ(AᵀA)v

So all the stretching is stored in AᵀA. A small check with slide 1's matrix, for v = 10:

A = 2−211:   Av = 21,   |Av|² = 4 + 1 = 5
AᵀA = 5−3−35:   vᵀ(AᵀA)v = top-left entry = 5 ✓

AᵀA is symmetric, since (AᵀA)ᵀ = AᵀA. So its eigenvectors are perpendicular (lesson 7). For a unit eigenvector v with eigenvalue λ, vᵀAᵀAv = λ, and this equals |Av|². So λ is never negative, and √λ is the stretch along v.

Why the eigenvectors of AᵀA are the right v's

A = UΣVᵀ,   so Aᵀ = VΣᵀUᵀ
AᵀA = VΣᵀUᵀUΣVᵀ = V(ΣᵀΣ)Vᵀ,   because UᵀU = I
This is QΛQᵀ from lesson 7 with Q = V and Λ = ΣᵀΣ = diag(σ₁², σ₂², …). (Σ is rectangular when A is, so we write ΣᵀΣ, a square diagonal matrix.)

So the eigenvectors of AᵀA give V, and the roots of its eigenvalues give the σ's. Then uᵢ = Avᵢ/σᵢ for each nonzero σᵢ. (If σᵢ = 0, any unit vectors that complete U to an orthogonal matrix will do.) For 2 by 2, σ₁σ₂ = |det A|, because turns don't change area.

The recipe, worked through

A = 2−211 (slide 1),   AᵀA = 5−3−35
trace 10, det 25 − 9 = 16:   λ² − 10λ + 16 = (λ − 8)(λ − 2),   λ = 8 and 2
σ₁ = √8 = 2√2 with v₁ = 1−1/√2,   σ₂ = √2 with v₂ = 11/√2 ✓ (as found by turning v on slide 1)
u₁ = Av₁/σ₁ = 2√20/(2√2) = 10,   u₂ = 0√2/√2 = 01
Check: σ₁σ₂ = 2√2 · √2 = 4 = |det A| = |2 + 2| ✓

Not the eigenvalues of A

For a symmetric matrix with positive eigenvalues, singular values and eigenvalues match. In general they don't. Take the shear from the code below:

A = 1101:   both eigenvalues are 1
AᵀA = 1112:   trace 3, det 1,   λ = (3 ± √5)/2 ≈ 2.618 and 0.382
σ₁ = √2.618 ≈ 1.618,   σ₂ = √0.382 ≈ 0.618.   Check: 1.618 · 0.618 ≈ 1 = det A ✓

Singular values give the largest and smallest stretch overall. Eigenvalues only describe the special directions that stay on their own line.
