Strang §7.3 · Principal component analysis

PCA: finding the main direction of data

Slide 4 kept the biggest piece of a picture. Now we keep the biggest piece of a cloud of measured points. Picture the GPS dots your phone records as you walk down a long straight street. The dots wobble a little to the sides, but together they form a long, thin cloud along the street. The street's direction is the cloud's main direction. Principal component analysis (PCA) finds it from the numbers alone. Put the points in the rows of a matrix, move the cloud so its average sits at the origin, and take the SVD. The first input direction v₁ points along the cloud, and σ₁² says how much of the spread lies that way: the sum of the squared distances of the points from the average, measured along that direction.

Lesson 8 · slide 5PCA
center: C = data − mean    C = UΣVᵀ    first principal component = v₁    share = σ₁² / (σ₁² + σ₂² + …)
Key ideas
  1. Put the measurements as rows of a matrix and subtract the average of each column: the centered matrix C.
  2. The first principal component is v₁, the first input direction in the SVD of C. It is the direction of greatest spread.
  3. σᵢ² measures the spread along vᵢ; σ₁² / (σ₁² + σ₂² + …) is the share it explains.
  4. The line through the mean along v₁ minimises the sum of squared perpendicular distances to the points.

A cloud with a main direction

A cloud of points can be round, or long and thin. A long cloud has a direction where it spreads the most. That direction is what PCA is looking for.

Step 1: center the data

Take five points: 11, 20, 32, 44 and 53. Their average is

x: (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5)/5 = 3,   y: (1 + 0 + 2 + 4 + 3)/5 = 2,   mean = 32

Subtract 32 from every point, so the cloud sits around the origin. The centered points are the rows of a 5 by 2 matrix C.

Step 2: the SVD of C

C = −2−1−1−2001221,   CᵀC = 4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 42 + 2 + 0 + 2 + 22 + 2 + 0 + 2 + 21 + 4 + 0 + 4 + 1 = 108810
trace 20, det 100 − 64 = 36:   λ² − 20λ + 36 = (λ − 18)(λ − 2),   λ = 18 and 2
σ₁ = √18 ≈ 4.24 with v₁ = 11/√2,   σ₂ = √2 ≈ 1.41 with v₂ = 1−1/√2
Share along v₁: 18/(18 + 2) = 90%

Why v₁ is the main direction

For a unit direction v, the vector Cv lists how far each point reaches along v. Then ‖Cv‖² adds up the squares: the spread along v. By slide 1, ‖Cv‖ is largest for v = v₁, with ‖Cv₁‖ = σ₁. Check with our points:

Cv₁ = −3−3033/√2,   ‖Cv₁‖² = (9 + 9 + 0 + 9 + 9)/2 = 18 = σ₁² ✓
Cv₂ = −110−11/√2,   ‖Cv₂‖² = 4/2 = 2 = σ₂² ✓

So v₁ is the first principal component. The line through the mean 32 in the direction 11 is y = x − 1.

Not the same as least squares

Lesson 5 fitted a line by making the vertical gaps small. That assumes only y has errors. PCA treats x and y the same and makes the perpendicular distances small. Take the centered point 12 and drop it straight onto the main line:

(part along the line)² + (gap)² = 4.5 + 0.5 = 5 = 1² + 2²   (Pythagoras)

Each point's squared distance from the mean is fixed. So the more spread a line keeps along itself, the smaller the gaps to it. For our five points the gaps squared add up to σ₂² = 2: the total spread is 20 (the trace of CᵀC), the line keeps 18, and 20 − 18 = 2 is left over. That is the smallest possible.
