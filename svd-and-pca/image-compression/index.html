Strang §7.1 · Image processing by linear algebra

Compressing an image with the SVD

Slide 3 split a matrix into rank-one pieces, loudest first. Now we use that on photos. A grey photo is just a big table of numbers, one per pixel, so it is a matrix. Think of a painter. First she blocks in the big areas, a dark sky and a light field. Then she adds the smaller shapes, and the fine detail comes last. The SVD splits a photo into layers in the same order: the first pieces carry the big shapes and the last ones carry tiny detail. Keep the first few pieces and throw the rest away. The picture still looks right, and you store far fewer numbers. That is image compression.

Lesson 8 · slide 4Image compression
A ≈ A_k = σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ + … + σₖuₖvₖᵀ    error set by the dropped σ's
Key ideas
  1. A grey image with m rows and n columns of pixels is an m by n matrix.
  2. A_k, the first k SVD pieces, is the closest matrix of rank k to A (the Eckart–Young theorem).
  3. Storing A_k needs k(m + n + 1) numbers instead of mn.
  4. Fast-falling singular values mean the image compresses well.

Pictures are matrices

Give each pixel a number from 0 (white) to 1 (black). A picture of three stripes, black, grey and white, with 4 pixels in each row, is a 3 by 4 matrix. Each row repeats one number, so the whole flag is a column of stripe values times a row of ones: one rank-one piece.

10.50 1111 = 11110.50.50.50.50000
12 pixels, but only 3 + 4 = 7 numbers to store

A 1000 by 1000 photo is a million numbers. Real photos are not one piece, but they are close to a sum of not too many pieces.

Keep k pieces

A_k = σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ + σ₂u₂v₂ᵀ + … + σₖuₖvₖᵀ
Each piece costs m + n + 1 numbers (u, v and σ)
1000 by 1000 photo, k = 50: 50 · 2001 ≈ 100,000 numbers instead of 1,000,000

No matrix of rank k is closer to A than A_k. The error comes only from the dropped pieces: in any direction the copy is off by at most σₖ₊₁, the largest σ dropped. Small dropped σ's mean a good copy.

There is also a quick way to see how much each piece holds. Add up the squares of all the pixel values. That total equals σ₁² + σ₂² + σ₃² + …. (It is the trace of AᵀA, and lesson 7 showed that the trace is the sum of the eigenvalues.) So σ₁² out of the total is the share that piece 1 carries.

A worked example: a 4 by 4 block

Here is a tiny 4 by 4 corner of a picture: a black top half and a black block at the bottom left.

B = 1111111111001100:   only two different columns, 1111 and 1100, so rank 2
Sum of squares: 12 black pixels, so σ₁² + σ₂² = 12
σ₁ = 1 + √5 ≈ 3.24,   σ₂ = √5 − 1 ≈ 1.24.   Check: (6 + 2√5) + (6 − 2√5) = 12 ✓
Piece 1 carries σ₁²/12 = 10.47/12 ≈ 87% of the picture

For a block this small, two pieces cost 2 · (4 + 4 + 1) = 18 numbers, more than the 16 pixels. The saving only shows up for big pictures, where k is tiny compared with m and n.
