Strang §4.4 · Orthonormal bases

Orthonormal vectors and Q: the easiest basis to work with.

Vectors that are perpendicular to each other and have length 1 are orthonormal. Put them in the columns of a matrix Q and QᵀQ = I. With such a basis, coordinates are plain dot products, and projections and least squares need no inverse.

Lesson 5 · slide 6Orthonormal bases
QᵀQ = I    square Q: Q⁻¹ = Qᵀ    ‖Qx‖ = ‖x‖
Key ideas
  1. q₁, ..., qₙ are orthonormal when qᵢᵀqⱼ = 0 for i ≠ j and qᵢᵀqᵢ = 1.
  2. With those vectors as columns, QᵀQ = I.
  3. A square Q with QᵀQ = I is an orthogonal matrix. Its inverse is its transpose. (The name is a historical quirk: the columns are orthonormal, not just orthogonal.)
  4. Q keeps lengths and angles: rotations and reflections.
  5. Coordinates in an orthonormal basis are dot products: x = (q₁ᵀx)q₁ + ... + (qₙᵀx)qₙ.

A city with a perfect grid

In a city where the streets meet at right angles and every block is exactly 1 km, you can read your position directly from the street signs: 3 blocks east, 2 blocks north. In a city with slanted streets and uneven blocks, finding your coordinates means solving equations.

An orthonormal basis is the perfect grid. "Ortho" means perpendicular, and "normal" means length 1.

QᵀQ = I

Put the vectors in the columns of Q. Entry (i, j) of QᵀQ is the dot product qᵢᵀqⱼ. Perpendicular columns put zeros off the diagonal, and length 1 puts ones on it:

q₁ = (0.6, 0.8),   q₂ = (−0.8, 0.6)
q₁ · q₁ = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1,   q₂ · q₂ = 1,   q₁ · q₂ = −0.48 + 0.48 = 0
QᵀQ = 1001

When Q is square, QᵀQ = I says that Qᵀ is the inverse of Q. Inverting an orthogonal matrix costs nothing: just transpose it.

Lengths are kept

‖Qx‖² = (Qx)ᵀ(Qx) = xᵀQᵀQx = xᵀx = ‖x‖². An orthogonal matrix moves vectors without stretching them. In the lab, the unit square stays a unit square; it only turns. Rotations and reflections are the orthogonal matrices of the plane.
