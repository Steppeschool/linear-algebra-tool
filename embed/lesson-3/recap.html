Key ideas
  1. A⁻¹ undoes A. A square matrix has one exactly when all its pivots are nonzero; for 2 by 2, exactly when ad − bc ≠ 0.
  2. For 2 by 2: swap, flip signs, divide by ad − bc. In general: Gauss-Jordan on [A | I].
  3. (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹ and (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ: the order reverses.
  4. Symmetric means S = Sᵀ, and AᵀA always is.
  5. Permutations reorder rows, P⁻¹ = Pᵀ, and PA = LU.
  • §2.5 The inverse. Like changing money and changing it back: A⁻¹ turns every output Ax back into x, so A⁻¹A = I and Ax = b is solved by x = A⁻¹b. A matrix that sends two inputs to the same output has no inverse. That happens exactly when a pivot is zero, or one column is a combination of the others.
  • §2.5 The 2 by 2 formula. Swap a and d, flip the signs of b and c, divide by ad − bc. That number is the determinant, the yes-or-no number for a 2 by 2 inverse: ad − bc = 0 means the columns lie on one line and there is no inverse.
  • §2.5 Inverse of a product. Socks on, shoes on; shoes off, socks off. AB does B first, so (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹. Undoing lesson 2's elimination steps in reverse order gives L.
  • §2.5 Gauss-Jordan. Write down every move as you solve the puzzle: do row steps on [A | I] until the left half is I. The right half has recorded the steps, and it reads A⁻¹. A zero row on the left means no inverse. To solve Ax = b, elimination is still faster than computing A⁻¹.
  • §2.7 Transposes. Turn the spreadsheet: rows become columns, and an m by n matrix becomes n by m. The dot product is xᵀy, and (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ. A distance table is symmetric, S = Sᵀ, and AᵀA always is.
  • §2.7 Permutations. A new playlist order: P is I with its rows reordered, and PA reorders the rows of A. Sending every row back is the transpose, so P⁻¹ = Pᵀ. Doing the row exchanges first gives PA = LU.
