Strang §2.4 · Rules for matrix operations

Multiplying two matrices

Slide 5 multiplied an elimination matrix E by A with one rule: each row of E combines the rows of A. Now we see how any matrix times any matrix works. Lesson 1 already multiplied a matrix by a vector, and that is almost all we need. The idea is simple: B is just a few vectors standing side by side, its columns. Multiply A by each column of B, and stand the answers side by side. That is AB. To get any single number in AB, take a row of A and a column of B and find their dot product. Same rules as lesson 1, done several times.

Lesson 2 · slide 6Matrix times matrix
(AB)ij = (row i of A) · (column j of B)     AB = [ Ab₁   Ab₂ ], where b₁, b₂ are the columns of B
Key ideas
  1. One entry: the number in row i, column j of AB is row i of A dotted with column j of B.
  2. One column: column j of AB is A times column j of B, a combination of the columns of A.
  3. Sizes: (m × n) times (n × p) gives m × p. The inside numbers must match.
  4. Order matters: usually AB ≠ BA.

One number at a time: row times column

Take

A = 1234    B = 5678

The top-left number of AB comes from row 1 of A and column 1 of B. Multiply matching parts and add, a dot product (lesson 1, slide 2):

row 1 · column 1 = 12 · 57 = 1·5 + 2·7 = 5 + 14 = 19

Do the same for every pair. Row i and column j make the number in row i, column j:

row 1 · column 2 = 1·6 + 2·8 = 22
row 2 · column 1 = 3·5 + 4·7 = 43
row 2 · column 2 = 3·6 + 4·8 = 50
AB = 19224350

One column at a time: A times each column of B

Now look at a whole column of AB. Column 1 of AB is just A times column 1 of B, a matrix times a vector, exactly as in lesson 1. By columns:

A·57 = 5·13 + 7·24 = 5 + 1415 + 28 = 1943

That is column 1 of AB. Column 2 is A·68 = 2250. So every column of AB is a combination of the columns of A.

One row at a time

There is a mirror image of this. Row 1 of AB is row 1 of A times B: take 1 of B's first row plus 2 of B's second row:

1·56 + 2·78 = 1922

All three views add up the same products. Use row times column to compute by hand. Use the column view to understand what AB is.

The same with 3 × 3 matrices

Nothing new happens with bigger matrices. Each dot product just has three products to add instead of two. Watch all nine numbers of AB fill in:

A = 120013201    B = 102210041
row 1 · column 1 = 1·1 + 2·2 + 0·0 = 5    row 2 · column 2 = 0·0 + 1·1 + 3·4 = 13
AB = 5222133245

Not every pair can be multiplied

A row of A must have as many numbers as a column of B, or the dot product doesn't work. So the number of columns of A must equal the number of rows of B. If they don't match, AB simply does not exist:

(m × n) · (n × p) → (m × p)    the inside numbers match, the outside numbers give the size of AB
(2 × 3) · (3 × 2) → 2 × 2 ✓    (3 × 2) · (2 × 4) → 3 × 4 ✓
(2 × 3) · (2 × 3): a row of A has 3 numbers, a column of B has only 2. ✗ No product.

So with A = 123456, the product AA does not exist, even though A times itself sounds natural. Only square matrices (same number of rows and columns) can always be multiplied by themselves. And sometimes AB exists but BA doesn't: a 2 × 3 times a 3 × 4 works, but 3 × 4 times 2 × 3 does not.

A vector is a matrix with one column, so lesson 1's Ax is the case p = 1.

Order matters

Multiply the same two matrices the other way round:

BA = 5678 1234 = 23343146    but AB = 19224350

Different. With numbers, 3·5 = 5·3. With matrices, usually AB ≠ BA, so always keep the order. Other rules do carry over: (AB)C = A(BC), so the brackets can move, and A(B + C) = AB + AC (add matrices entry by entry).

A bigger example: 2 × 3 times 3 × 2

A = 120013    B = 102104
row 1 · column 1 = 1·1 + 2·2 + 0·0 = 5    row 1 · column 2 = 1·0 + 2·1 + 0·4 = 2
row 2 · column 1 = 0·1 + 1·2 + 3·0 = 2    row 2 · column 2 = 0·0 + 1·1 + 3·4 = 13
AB = 52213    (2 × 3) · (3 × 2) → (2 × 2)

Each dot product now has 3 products to add. A neural network layer is one big matrix product like this, with thousands of rows and columns.

Where this is used

Slide 5 already used it: each elimination step is a matrix, and the whole elimination is their product. The next slide turns that product around into A = LU. Graphics, robotics and machine learning chain matrices the same way.

Next: Elimination turned A into the triangle U. Can we get A back from U? Undoing the steps, as a product of matrices, gives the factorization A = LU.
