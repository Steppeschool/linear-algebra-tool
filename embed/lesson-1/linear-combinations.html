Strang §1.1 · Linear combinations

Linear algebra starts with cv + dw.

Take two vectors, multiply each by a number, and add. That is a linear combination. The whole course grows from one question: which points can a combination reach, and with which numbers?

Lesson 1 · slide 1Linear combinations
c 21 + d -11 = 2c − dc + d
Key ideas
  1. A vector in the plane is a pair of numbers, v = v₁v₂. We draw it as an arrow from the origin.
  2. Two operations: multiply a vector by a number c, and add two vectors. Both work one component at a time.
  3. Doing both gives a linear combination cv + dw.
  4. The big question: which vectors can we reach with all possible c and d?

Why vectors at all?

A sensor reports two numbers: 2 metres east and 1 metre north. They belong together, so we keep them as one pair: v = 21. That pair is a vector.

To add two vectors, add first parts to first parts and second parts to second parts. They never mix:

v = 21,   w = −11  →  v + w = (2 + (−1), 1 + 1) = 12

On the plane, adding means walking: go along v, then along w from its tip. You end at the tip of v + w.

Multiplying by a number

To get 3v, multiply each part by 3: 3v = 63. Same direction, three times as long. The 3 is called a scalar, because it only scales.

A negative number turns the arrow around: −v = −2−1. And 0·v is the zero vector 00.

Putting both together

Pick numbers c and d and form cv + dw. With c = 3 and d = 2:

3v + 2w = 63 + −22 = 45

That is a linear combination: "take 3 steps of v, then 2 steps of w".

The question behind everything

We ask this all course long: which vectors can we reach?

The multiples cv of one vector fill a line through the origin. That is where "linear" comes from. Two vectors that point different ways, like v and w, fill the whole plane.

The reverse is harder. Given a target like 45, what are c and d? Write the combination out:

c(2, 1) + d(−1, 1) = 2c − dc + d

We need 2c − d = 4 and c + d = 5 at the same time. Slide 4 and lesson 2 show how to solve that.

Example 3 · When the plane is not filled

Take v = 12 and w = 24. Notice that w = 2v: both arrows lie on the same line.

Show solutionHide solution
cv + dw = c(1, 2) + 2d(1, 2) = (c + 2d)·12

Every combination is a multiple of 12, so the combinations only fill a line, not the plane. The target 10 can never be reached.

Example 4

Find c and d with c·12 + d·01 = 38.

The first component gives c = 3. The second: 2·3 + d = 8, so d = 2.

Example 5

Can combinations of 24 and −1−2 ever reach 10?

Show solutionHide solution

No. −1−2 = −½·24, so both vectors lie on the line y = 2x. Every combination stays on that line, and 10 is not on it.
